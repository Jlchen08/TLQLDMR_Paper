\begin{abstract}
Accurate wind power forecasting under extreme weather conditions is critical for ensuring power grid stability, yet remains fundamentally challenging due to the inherent scarcity of extreme event samples and the substantial distribution shift between normal and anomalous meteorological regimes. While kernel-based methods—particularly Support Vector Regression (SVR) and Large Margin Distribution Machines (LDMR)—have demonstrated strong theoretical foundations and competitive performance in conventional forecasting scenarios, they suffer from three critical limitations when applied to extreme weather prediction: (1) the i.i.d. assumption fails under severe distribution shifts, (2) symmetric error regularization conflicts with the asymmetric nature of quantile-based interval forecasting, and (3) existing formulations lack mechanisms to leverage knowledge transfer from abundant normal weather data to scarce extreme weather samples.

To address these limitations, we propose a novel Transfer Learning Quantile Large Margin Distribution Machine for Regression (TLQLDMR), which extends the classical LDMR framework with three key innovations tailored for cross-domain extreme weather forecasting. First, we introduce an Asymmetric Variance Regularizer that reconciles distributional robustness with quantile estimation by decomposing residual variance into quantile-specific components, thereby preserving the requisite skewness for accurate interval prediction while maintaining margin concentration. Second, we develop a Supervised Transfer Learning framework that integrates Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) for feature-space alignment with a Weighted Pinball Loss mechanism for label-space adaptation, strategically assigning higher weights to scarce target domain samples as corrective anchors. Third, we propose a scalable Nystr\"{o}m-SGD optimization algorithm that reduces computational complexity from $O(N^3)$ to $O(T \cdot N \cdot m)$, enabling practical deployment on large-scale wind farm datasets. Extensive experiments on six wind farms demonstrate that TLQLDMR consistently outperforms both classical kernel-based baselines (SVR, SVQR, LDMR) and state-of-the-art transfer learning methods, achieving 12--18\% RMSE reduction and superior 95\% interval coverage (94.8\%) with optimal reliability-sharpness balance. Comprehensive ablation studies confirm the necessity of each component, establishing TLQLDMR as a principled extension of the LDMR paradigm for transfer learning scenarios.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The global transition toward renewable energy has positioned wind power as a cornerstone of sustainable electricity generation, with installed capacity projected to exceed 1,000 GW by 2030 \citep{wang2019review}. However, the inherent intermittency and volatility of wind resources pose significant challenges to power grid stability and dispatch optimization. Accurate wind power forecasting is therefore critical for balancing supply and demand, reducing operational costs, and ensuring reliable grid integration \citep{wang2019review, zhang2024kde}. While substantial progress has been achieved in short-term forecasting under normal meteorological conditions, prediction performance degrades dramatically during extreme weather events—such as typhoons, cold waves, or heat waves—when wind power fluctuations are most severe and grid stability is most vulnerable \citep{s41597-022-01696-6}.

Extreme weather events present a dual challenge for data-driven forecasting models. First, such events are inherently rare, resulting in severe data scarcity: extreme samples typically constitute less than 5\% of historical records \citep{s41597-022-01696-6}. Second, the meteorological dynamics during extreme conditions differ fundamentally from normal regimes, inducing substantial distribution shifts in both feature space (e.g., wind speed, temperature) and label space (power output). Conventional machine learning models, which assume that training and testing data are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.), fail catastrophically under such distribution mismatches. For instance, random forest models trained on normal weather data exhibit root mean squared error (RMSE) increases exceeding 40\% when applied to extreme weather scenarios \citep{mustaffa2025rf, chen2025brf}.

\subsection{Kernel-Based Methods for Wind Power Forecasting: Strengths and Limitations}

Among the diverse landscape of forecasting methodologies, kernel-based approaches—particularly Support Vector Regression (SVR) and its variants—have established themselves as a theoretically principled and empirically competitive paradigm. Unlike black-box deep learning models that require massive training data and lack interpretability, kernel methods offer several distinctive advantages: (1) strong generalization guarantees rooted in statistical learning theory \citep{meena2025flsvr}, (2) effective handling of high-dimensional feature spaces through the kernel trick without explicit feature engineering, (3) convex optimization formulations that guarantee global optima, and (4) robust performance on small-to-medium-sized datasets where deep models tend to overfit \citep{wang2024ssvqr}.

Traditional SVR minimizes the $\epsilon$-insensitive loss to estimate the conditional mean of the target variable, achieving robust point predictions under Gaussian noise assumptions. However, for wind power forecasting under extreme conditions, point estimates are insufficient—grid operators require probabilistic interval forecasts that quantify prediction uncertainty \citep{hu2025nescqr, hu2025ncqgbr}. To address this need, Support Vector Quantile Regression (SVQR) extends SVR by replacing the symmetric $\epsilon$-loss with the asymmetric pinball loss, enabling estimation of conditional quantiles (e.g., 5th and 95th percentiles for prediction intervals) \citep{ye2025nfs, wang2024ssvqr}. Recent advances in SVQR include twin formulations for improved computational efficiency \citep{tsvqr2023}, sparse regularization for feature selection \citep{wang2024ssvqr}, and nonlinear feature selection mechanisms \citep{ye2025nfs}.

Despite these advances, both SVR and SVQR share a fundamental limitation: they optimize only the minimum margin (the distance from the decision boundary to the closest support vectors) while ignoring the statistical distribution of margins across the entire dataset. This focus on worst-case samples can lead to overfitting and poor generalization, particularly when training data contains outliers or label noise. To overcome this limitation, the Large Margin Distribution Machine (LDMR) paradigm was introduced, which explicitly optimizes the first-order and second-order statistics of the margin distribution—maximizing the mean margin while minimizing the margin variance \citep{zhang2019optimal, gupta2021least}. By concentrating prediction errors tightly around zero, LDMR achieves superior generalization compared to classical SVR on a wide range of regression tasks \citep{gupta2021least, hazarika2023mode}. Variants of LDMR have been successfully applied to wind turbine fault detection \citep{tang2019cost}, imbalanced classification \citep{CHENG2016107, cheng2016cost}, and structural health monitoring \citep{doi:10.1177/14759217231159948}.

However, when we examine the application of these kernel-based methods to wind power forecasting under extreme weather conditions, three critical gaps emerge:

\textbf{Gap 1: Inability to handle distribution shift.} Both SVR/SVQR and LDMR are predicated on the i.i.d. assumption—they assume that training and testing data are drawn from the same distribution. This assumption is fundamentally violated during extreme weather events, where the joint distribution $P(\boldsymbol{X}, Y)$ differs drastically between normal (source domain) and extreme (target domain) conditions. A model trained solely on abundant normal weather data will exhibit severe performance degradation when deployed under extreme conditions, regardless of how well the margin distribution is optimized. While transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques have been extensively studied in computer vision \citep{TAO20123962} and sensor drift compensation \citep{YAN2022107664, GUO2022118237, dp2, ss2}, their integration with kernel-based regression methods for wind power forecasting remains largely unexplored.

\textbf{Gap 2: Conflict between symmetric variance regularization and asymmetric quantile estimation.} The core principle of LDMR—minimizing the variance of prediction residuals—implicitly assumes a symmetric error distribution centered at zero. This is appropriate for conditional mean estimation but fundamentally incompatible with quantile regression. For example, when estimating the 95th percentile (upper prediction bound), the optimal residual distribution should be asymmetric, with 95\% of errors being negative (under-predictions) and only 5\% positive (over-predictions). Naively applying LDMR's symmetric variance minimization to quantile regression will force the error distribution toward symmetry, thereby degrading interval calibration and violating the target coverage probability. To the best of our knowledge, no existing work has addressed this theoretical tension between distributional robustness and quantile estimation within the LDMR framework.

\textbf{Gap 3: Neglect of label information in transfer learning.} Existing transfer learning approaches for wind power forecasting predominantly adopt unsupervised domain adaptation strategies, focusing exclusively on aligning feature distributions (e.g., via Maximum Mean Discrepancy) while treating target domain labels—even when available in small quantities—as entirely unused supervision signals \citep{TAO20123962, GUO2022118237}. This is particularly problematic for extreme weather forecasting, where scarce target samples represent high-value data points that capture critical domain-specific dynamics. A principled transfer learning framework should leverage both feature-space alignment and label-space supervision, strategically weighting target samples to guide model adaptation.

\subsection{Our Contribution: TLQLDMR}

To bridge these gaps, we propose the Transfer Learning Quantile Large Margin Distribution Machine for Regression (TLQLDMR), a principled extension of the classical LDMR paradigm specifically designed for cross-domain wind power forecasting under extreme weather conditions. Our approach preserves the theoretical strengths of kernel-based methods—convex optimization, generalization guarantees, and robustness on limited data—while introducing three key innovations:

\textbf{Innovation 1: Asymmetric Variance Regularizer for Quantile-Aware Margin Distribution.} We introduce a novel asymmetric variance regularization term that decomposes the residual variance into quantile-specific components. Unlike standard LDMR, which imposes symmetric variance minimization $\text{Var}(y_i - f(\boldsymbol{x}_i))$, our formulation applies differential regularization strengths to positive and negative residuals, thereby compressing overall error dispersion while preserving the requisite skewness for accurate quantile prediction. This reconciles the tension between distributional robustness (tight margin concentration) and quantile estimation (asymmetric error distribution), enabling TLQLDMR to achieve both superior point prediction accuracy and well-calibrated prediction intervals.

\textbf{Innovation 2: Supervised Transfer Learning with Dual-Space Adaptation.} We develop a unified transfer learning framework that integrates two complementary adaptation mechanisms. In the feature space, we employ Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) minimization to align the source (normal weather) and target (extreme weather) distributions in the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS), ensuring that the learned feature representation is domain-invariant \citep{TAO20123962}. Crucially, in the label space, we introduce a Weighted Pinball Loss mechanism that assigns substantially higher weights to scarce target domain samples, treating them as high-value corrective anchors that guide the model toward extreme weather dynamics. This dual-space adaptation strategy enables TLQLDMR to leverage abundant source data for structural learning while prioritizing target data for domain-specific refinement, addressing the label neglect problem in existing unsupervised domain adaptation methods.

\textbf{Innovation 3: Scalable Nystr\"{o}m-SGD Optimization for Large-Scale Deployment.} The standard quadratic programming (QP) formulation of kernel-based transfer learning incurs $O(N^3)$ computational complexity due to the need to compute and invert the full kernel matrix, rendering it impractical for large-scale wind farm datasets with tens of thousands of samples. We propose a scalable optimization algorithm that combines Nystr\"{o}m approximation—which constructs an explicit low-rank feature mapping using $m \ll N$ landmark points—with stochastic gradient descent (SGD). This reduces complexity to $O(T \cdot N \cdot m)$, where $T$ is the number of iterations, enabling training to complete within seconds on commodity hardware while maintaining prediction accuracy within 0.5\% of the exact QP solution.

\subsection{Experimental Validation and Key Findings}

We conduct extensive experiments on a publicly available multi-site wind farm SCADA dataset \citep{s41597-022-01696-6}, encompassing six wind farms with capacities ranging from 36 MW to 200 MW. Extreme weather events are rigorously defined using statistical thresholds (98th percentile wind speed, 2nd/98th percentile temperature) and physical constraints (cut-out wind speed $>$ 25 m/s). Our evaluation compares TLQLDMR against three categories of baselines: (1) classical kernel-based methods (SVR, SVQR, LDMR), (2) ensemble methods (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting), and (3) state-of-the-art transfer learning approaches (TrAdaBoost, domain adaptation SVR). The results demonstrate:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Superior deterministic prediction within the kernel-based paradigm}: TLQLDMR achieves 12--18\% RMSE reduction compared to non-transfer kernel baselines (SVR, SVQR, LDMR) and 6--9\% improvement over transfer learning baselines on the wind farm exhibiting the most severe distribution shift. This validates that our innovations—asymmetric variance regularization and supervised transfer learning—effectively extend the LDMR framework to handle cross-domain scenarios.

    \item \textbf{State-of-the-art probabilistic interval prediction}: At the 95\% confidence level, TLQLDMR is the only method that simultaneously achieves target-level coverage (94.8\%) and the lowest Coverage Width-based Criterion (CWC), indicating optimal reliability-sharpness balance. Classical SVQR under-covers (88--91\%) due to distribution shift, while ensemble methods produce excessively wide intervals. This demonstrates the effectiveness of our asymmetric variance regularizer in maintaining quantile calibration under domain shift.

    \item \textbf{Robustness to measurement noise}: Under injected Gaussian noise with signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) ranging from 20 dB to 5 dB, TLQLDMR maintains stable interval coverage (93--95\%) while baselines degrade to 85--88\%. This robustness stems from the margin distribution optimization principle inherited from LDMR, which concentrates errors tightly even under noisy conditions.

    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency for practical deployment}: The Nystr\"{o}m-SGD algorithm reduces training time from 47 minutes (standard QP solver) to 8 seconds on a dataset with 50,000 samples, achieving a 350$\times$ speedup with negligible accuracy loss ($<$0.5\% RMSE increase). This makes TLQLDMR practical for operational wind farm forecasting systems that require frequent model retraining.
\end{itemize}

Comprehensive ablation studies confirm the necessity of each component: removing transfer learning, asymmetric variance regularization, or MMD alignment degrades either deterministic accuracy, interval calibration, or both. Residual density analysis further reveals that TLQLDMR produces well-calibrated error distributions that closely match the target quantile, whereas baselines exhibit systematic bias.

\subsection{Positioning and Scope}

It is important to clarify the scope and positioning of our work. TLQLDMR is not designed to compete with large-scale deep learning models (e.g., LSTM, Transformer, CNN-based architectures) that have demonstrated impressive performance on massive wind power datasets with millions of samples \citep{wang2019review, hybriddl2025, amqrnn2025}. Rather, our contribution lies in advancing the kernel-based regression paradigm—specifically the LDMR framework—to handle transfer learning scenarios that are prevalent in extreme weather forecasting. Kernel methods offer distinct advantages in settings where: (1) training data is limited (hundreds to tens of thousands of samples), (2) theoretical guarantees and interpretability are valued, (3) computational resources are constrained, and (4) convex optimization is preferred over non-convex deep learning. By demonstrating that classical kernel-based methods can be effectively extended with transfer learning capabilities, we provide practitioners with a principled alternative to deep learning that is particularly well-suited for extreme weather forecasting where data scarcity is the primary bottleneck.

\section{Related Work}\label{rel}
This section briefly reviews the theoretical foundations related to the proposed method, including Support Vector Quantile Regression, Large Margin Distribution Machine for Regression, and Transfer Learning with Maximum Mean Discrepancy.

\subsection{Support Vector Quantile Regression (SVQR)}
Traditional Support Vector Regression (SVR) relies on the $\epsilon$-insensitive loss function, which assumes a symmetric error distribution (e.g., Gaussian) and estimates the conditional mean of the target variable. However, in scenarios like partial shading or wind curtailment, the noise distribution is often asymmetric and heteroscedastic. To address this, Support Vector Quantile Regression (SVQR) was introduced to estimate conditional quantiles, providing a comprehensive view of the outcome distribution.

The core of SVQR is the non-symmetric pinball loss function, defined as:
\begin{equation}
L_{\tau}(u) = \begin{cases} 
\tau u, & u \ge 0 \\ 
(\tau - 1) u, & u < 0 ,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\tau \in (0,1)$ indicates the target quantile. Unlike SVR, SVQR finds a decision boundary that divides the data such that a proportion $\tau$ of the samples lie below the boundary. The optimization problem minimizes the structural risk along with the empirical risk measured by the pinball loss:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b} \frac{1}{2} \|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{n} L_{\tau}(y_i - (\boldsymbol{w}^T \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_i) + b)),
\end{equation}
this formulation allows SVQR to capture heavy-tailed distributions and outliers more effectively than mean-based regression.

\subsection{Large Margin Distribution Machine for Regression (LDMR)}
While SVR and SVQR focus on maximizing the minimum margin (the distance from the closest instances to the hyperplane), they largely ignore the statistical distribution of the margin over the entire dataset. The \textbf{Large Margin Distribution Machine (LDMR)} was proposed to improve generalization by explicitly optimizing the distribution of the margins (or residuals).

The key idea of LDMR is to not only maximize the margin of the support vectors but also to minimize the variance of the error distribution for all samples. A typical LDMR formulation for regression integrates two additional statistical regularization terms into the objective function:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ldmr_concept}
\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b} \frac{1}{2} \|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + \lambda_1 \text{Var}(\text{Residuals}) + \lambda_2 \text{Mean}(\text{Residuals}) + C \sum_{i=1}^{n} L(y_i, f(\boldsymbol{x}_i)),
\end{equation}
by constraining the variance, LDMR forces the prediction errors to be clustered tightly, leading to a more compact and robust hypothesis space. Our work extends this concept by modifying the symmetric variance term of standard LDMR to an asymmetric one suitable for quantile regression.

\subsection{Transfer Learning and Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)}
Standard machine learning models operate under the assumption that training (source) and testing (target) data are drawn from the same probability distribution. When this assumption is violated—such as in wind power forecasting across different weather regimes—the model performance degrades significantly. Transfer learning aims to transfer knowledge from a label-rich source domain to a target domain with different but related distributions.

A fundamental challenge in transfer learning is measuring and reducing the distribution discrepancy. Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is a widely used non-parametric metric that measures the distance between two distributions in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). Given source data $\{\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}\}$ and target data $\{\boldsymbol{x}_{T,j}\}$, the squared MMD distance is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\text{MMD}^2(D_S, D_T) = \left\| \frac{1}{n_S} \sum_{i=1}^{n_S} \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}) - \frac{1}{n_T} \sum_{j=1}^{n_T} \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{T,j}) \right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $\phi(\cdot)$ is the feature mapping induced by the kernel. By minimizing the MMD distance during training, the model learns a feature representation where the source and target domains are statistically aligned, enabling the classifier or regressor trained on the source domain to generalize well to the target domain.


\section{Proposed Methodology}\label{novel}
In this section, we elaborate on the construction of the Transfer Learning Quantile Regression Model (TLQLDMR). We first define the necessary notations, then present the final objective function which integrates Structural Risk, Distribution Variance, Domain Adaptation (MMD), and Weighted Pinball Loss. Finally, we describe the constraints introduced to handle the non-differentiability of the Quantile Loss.

\subsection{Notations}\label{not}
Consider the problem of wind power forecasting under extreme weather conditions. We define the \textbf{Source Domain (Normal Weather)} as $D_{S} = \{(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}, y_{S,i})\}_{i=1}^{n_S}$, containing abundant conventional weather samples, and the \textbf{Target Domain (Extreme Weather)} as $D_{T} = \{(\boldsymbol{x}_{T,j}, y_{T,j})\}_{j=1}^{n_T}$, containing scarce extreme weather samples. The input space $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ represents feature vectors constructed via sliding window processing, and the output space $\mathcal{Y} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ represents wind power. The sample sizes satisfy $n_T \ll n_S$.

Due to drastic meteorological changes during extreme events, the joint distributions differ significantly: $P_S(\boldsymbol{X}_S, \boldsymbol{Y}_S) \neq P_T(\boldsymbol{X}_T, \boldsymbol{Y}_T)$. This distribution discrepancy implies that a model trained solely on $P_S$ will fail on $P_T$.
We define a feature mapping $\phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{H}$ that maps the input to a high-dimensional Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). The prediction model is defined as:
\begin{equation}
f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{w}^T \phi(\boldsymbol{x}) + b,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{w}$ is the weight vector and $b$ is the bias. The quantile $\tau \in (0, 1)$ is set to a specific value (e.g., $\tau=0.95$) for interval prediction (e.g., predicting the upper bound).

\subsubsection{Objective Function}
Our optimization goal is to find the optimal $\boldsymbol{w}, b$ and slack variables $\boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\xi}^*$ to minimize the following objective function $J$. Let $\bar{u}_S = \frac{1}{n_S}\sum_{i=1}^{n_S}(y_{S,i} - f(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}))$ denote the mean residual over the source domain:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\xi}^*} \quad J = & \underbrace{\frac{1}{2} \|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2}_{\text{(1) Structural Risk}} \\
& + \underbrace{\lambda_1 \sum_{i=1}^{n_S} \left( (y_{S,i} - \boldsymbol{w}^T\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}) - b) - \bar{u}_S \right)^2}_{\text{(2) Distribution Variance (LDMR Core)}} \\
& + \underbrace{\lambda_2 \left\| \frac{1}{n_S} \sum_{i=1}^{n_S} \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}) - \frac{1}{n_T} \sum_{j=1}^{n_T} \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{T,j}) \right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2}_{\text{(3) Domain Adaptation (MMD)}} \\
& + \underbrace{C_S \sum_{i=1}^{n_S} L_{\tau}(y_{S,i}, f(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i})) + C_T \sum_{j=1}^{n_T} L_{\tau}(y_{T,j}, f(\boldsymbol{x}_{T,j}))}_{\text{(4) Weighted Pinball Loss}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
the physical meaning of each term is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Structural Risk}: This term controls the complexity of the model and prevents overfitting, embodying the maximum margin principle of SVM.
    \item \textbf{Distribution Variance}: This term is the core of our proposed Quantile LDMR (QLDMR), incorporating a critical modification to the original LDMR variance.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Theoretical Conflict}: The original LDMR minimizes the variance of absolute residuals, which assumes a symmetric error distribution suitable for mean regression. However, this symmetry contradicts the inherent asymmetry of Quantile Regression (governed by $\tau$). Applying a symmetric variance penalty would force the residual distribution towards the center, degrading the skewness required for accurate quantile estimation.
        \item \textbf{Asymmetric Modification}: To resolve this, we employ an \textbf{Asymmetric Variance} regularization based on the algebraic (signed) residuals. This design allows the model to reduce error dispersion (compactness) without constraining the distribution's direction, thereby preserving the non-symmetric properties essential for the Pinball Loss and improving interval coverage.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Domain Adaptation (MMD)}: In the context of wind power forecasting under extreme weather conditions, our primary objective is to mitigate the differences in data distributions across various domains by means of learning a feature transformation. Consequently, the effective measurement of distribution disparities between domains becomes a paramount concern. In this pursuit, it is crucial to select a method suitable for quantifying distribution dissimilarity, while ensuring that the chosen method is computationally efficient and easily integrated into the optimization problem of our model. Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) has been demonstrated as an effective approach for estimating the distance between two distributions, offering a relatively straightforward implementation with fast computation speed. It targets the input space $\mathcal{X}$ to minimize the distance between the distribution centers of the source and target domains.
    \item \textbf{Weighted Pinball Loss}: This term targets the output space $\mathcal{Y}$ and acts as the decisive component for ``Supervised Transfer Learning''. Unlike unsupervised transfer which relies solely on feature alignment, we explicitly incorporate the target domain samples $D_T$ into the loss calculation for the following reasons:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Corrective Anchors}: The target samples act as ``anchors for correction''. The source domain data ($D_S$) teaches the model general laws (e.g., higher wind speed leads to higher power), but extreme weather samples ($D_T$) often violate these laws (e.g., high wind speed causing cut-out and zero power). If $D_T$ were excluded from the loss, the model, driven implicitly by $D_S$, would wrongly predict full power during extreme events. Including $D_T$ in the loss imposes a heavy penalty for such mispredictions, forcing the model to learn the specific mapping of extreme events.
        \item \textbf{Differentiation from MMD}: While MMD brings the ``appearance'' (features) of normal and extreme weather closer, the Pinball Loss ensures the ``content'' (labels) is accurate. It forces the regression curve to correctly envelope the rare data points that MMD cannot correct on its own.
        \item \textbf{Asymmetric Weighted Strategy}: We employ a weighted strategy to address the extreme sample imbalance ($n_T \ll n_S$). By setting $C_T \gg C_S$ (e.g., $C_T = 100 C_S$), the optimization process assigns substantially higher importance to target domain samples. This weighting scheme ensures that prediction errors on scarce extreme weather data incur penalties commensurate with their practical significance, preventing them from being dominated by the abundant source domain samples during gradient updates.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Constraints}
To handle the non-differentiability of the Pinball Loss, we introduce slack variables $\xi_k, \xi_k^*$ to transform it into linear constraints. For every sample $k$ in the training set $D = D_S \cup D_T$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:constraints}
\begin{cases}
y_k - (\boldsymbol{w}^T\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_k) + b) \le \xi_k, & \forall k \in \{1, \dots, n_S + n_T\} \\
(\boldsymbol{w}^T\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_k) + b) - y_k \le \xi_k^*, & \forall k \in \{1, \dots, n_S + n_T\} \\
\xi_k \ge 0, \quad \xi_k^* \ge 0,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
in the objective function, the term $L_{\tau}(y_k, f(\boldsymbol{x}_k))$ corresponds to $\tau \xi_k + (1-\tau) \xi_k^*$.

\subsection{Model Optimization}\label{optimization}
In this section, we derive the dual problem of TLQLDMR to enable efficient solving using Quadratic Programming (QP).

\subsubsection{Matrix Reformulation of the Primal Problem}
To facilitate the derivation, we rewrite the variance term and the MMD term in the objective function [Eq. (\ref{eq:objective})] using matrix traces or norms. Let $X = [\boldsymbol{X}_S, \boldsymbol{X}_T]$ denote the entire training set with $N = n_S + n_T$ samples. 
For consistency with standard derivations, we add a coefficient of $1/2$ to the variance and MMD terms. The primal objective can be reformulated as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:primal_matrix}
\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\xi}^*} \quad \frac{1}{2} \|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + \frac{\lambda_1}{2} \text{Var}_S(\boldsymbol{w}, b) + \frac{\lambda_2}{2} \text{MMD}^2(\boldsymbol{w}) + \sum_{k=1}^N C_k L_\tau(y_k, f(\boldsymbol{x}_k))
\end{equation}
where $C_k$ takes value $C_S$ if sample $k \in D_S$ and $C_T$ if $k \in D_T$.

We introduce two auxiliary matrices to express the variance and MMD terms in Eq. (\ref{eq:primal_matrix}) as quadratic forms of the weights:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Variance Matrix } $\mathbf{S}$: To represent the source domain variance $\sum_{i \in D_S} (y_{S,i} - f(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}) - \bar{u}_S)^2$, we define a centering matrix $\boldsymbol{H}_S = \boldsymbol{I}_{n_S} - \frac{1}{n_S} \boldsymbol{1}_{n_S} \boldsymbol{1}_{n_S}^T$. The variance term can be written as $\| \boldsymbol{H}_S (\boldsymbol{y}_S - \boldsymbol{f}_S) \|^2$. To extend this to the full dataset, we define an $N \times N$ matrix $\mathbf{S}$:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:matrix_S}
    \mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{H}_S & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{MMD Matrix } $\mathbf{M}$: The MMD term represents the distance between means. We define the MMD coefficient matrix $\mathbf{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ as:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:matrix_M}
    M_{ij} = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{n_S^2} & i,j \in D_S \\ \frac{1}{n_T^2} & i,j \in D_T \\ \frac{-1}{n_S n_T} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Representer Theorem and Kernelization}
According to the Representer Theorem \cite{scholkopf2001representer}, the optimal weight vector $\boldsymbol{w}$ lies in the span of the feature mappings of the training samples:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:representer}
\boldsymbol{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \beta_i \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_i) = \boldsymbol{\Phi} \boldsymbol{\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\Phi} = [\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_1), \dots, \phi(\boldsymbol{x}_N)]$ is the feature matrix and $\boldsymbol{\beta} = [\beta_1, \dots, \beta_N]^T$ are the coefficients.
Substituting Eq. (\ref{eq:representer}) into the prediction function, we get $f(\boldsymbol{X}) = \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \boldsymbol{K} + b \boldsymbol{1}^T$, where $\boldsymbol{K} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}^T \boldsymbol{\Phi}$ is the $N \times N$ kernel matrix.
Consequently, using Eq. (\ref{eq:matrix_S}) and Eq. (\ref{eq:matrix_M}), the structural risk, variance, and MMD terms can be expressed in terms of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Structural Risk: $\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 = \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\beta}$.
    \item Distribution Variance: The quadratic part with respect to $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is $\boldsymbol{\beta}^T \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\beta}$. Note that the bias $b$ is eliminated by the centering matrix $\boldsymbol{H}_S$.
    \item Domain Adaptation (MMD): $\text{MMD}^2(\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{M} \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\beta}$.
\end{itemize}

Combining these, we define the total coefficient matrix $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Omega}
\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \boldsymbol{K} + \lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{K} + \lambda_2 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{M} \boldsymbol{K}
\end{equation}
By substituting the kernelized terms and Eq. (\ref{eq:Omega}) into Eq. (\ref{eq:primal_matrix}), the kernelized primal problem becomes (ignoring constant terms w.r.t $b$ in variance):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel_primal}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}, b, \boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\xi}^*} \quad \frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \boldsymbol{\Omega} \boldsymbol{\beta} - (\lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{Y})^T \boldsymbol{\beta} + \sum_{k=1}^N C_k (\tau \xi_k + (1-\tau)\xi_k^*)
\end{equation}
subject to the constraints from Eq. (\ref{eq:constraints}):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel_constraints}
y_k - (\boldsymbol{K}_k \boldsymbol{\beta} + b) \le \xi_k, \quad (\boldsymbol{K}_k \boldsymbol{\beta} + b) - y_k \le \xi_k^*, \quad \xi_k, \xi_k^* \ge 0
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Lagrangian and Dual Problem}
We verify the problem by introducing Lagrange multipliers $\alpha_k, \alpha_k^*, \mu_k, \mu_k^* \ge 0$ for Eq. (\ref{eq:kernel_constraints}). The Lagrangian function $\mathcal{L}$ is constructed as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lagrangian}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} = &\frac{1}{2} \boldsymbol{\beta}^T \boldsymbol{\Omega} \boldsymbol{\beta} - (\lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{Y})^T \boldsymbol{\beta} + \sum_{k=1}^N C_k (\tau \xi_k + (1-\tau)\xi_k^*) \\
&- \sum_{k=1}^N \alpha_k (\xi_k - y_k + \boldsymbol{K}_k \boldsymbol{\beta} + b) - \sum_{k=1}^N \alpha_k^* (\xi_k^* + y_k - \boldsymbol{K}_k \boldsymbol{\beta} - b) - \sum (\mu_k \xi_k + \mu_k^* \xi_k^*)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
By setting the partial derivatives of Eq. (\ref{eq:lagrangian}) with respect to the primal variables ($b, \boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\xi}^*, \boldsymbol{\beta}$) to zero, we obtain the KKT conditions:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:kkt_b} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = 0 & \implies \sum_{k=1}^N (\alpha_k - \alpha_k^*) = 0 \\
    \label{eq:kkt_xi} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \xi_k} = 0, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \xi_k^*} = 0 & \implies \alpha_k \in [0, C_k \tau], \quad \alpha_k^* \in [0, C_k (1-\tau)] \\
    \label{eq:kkt_beta} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\beta}} = 0 & \implies \boldsymbol{\Omega} \boldsymbol{\beta} - \lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{Y} - \boldsymbol{K} (\boldsymbol{\alpha} - \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*) = 0
\end{align}
Let $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \boldsymbol{\alpha} - \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*$. From Eq. (\ref{eq:kkt_beta}), the optimal $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is derived as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:beta_optimal}
\boldsymbol{\beta} = \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1} (\boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\gamma} + \lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{Y})
\end{equation}

Substituting Eq. (\ref{eq:beta_optimal}) back into Eq. (\ref{eq:lagrangian}), and simplifying using Eq. (\ref{eq:kkt_b}) and Eq. (\ref{eq:kkt_xi}), we arrive at the \textbf{Dual Problem}, which is a standard Quadratic Programming (QP) problem:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dual_problem}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*} \quad & \frac{1}{2} (\boldsymbol{\alpha} - \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*)^T \mathbf{Q}_{dual} (\boldsymbol{\alpha} - \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*) + \mathbf{p}_{dual}^T (\boldsymbol{\alpha} - \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_{k=1}^N (\alpha_k - \alpha_k^*) = 0 \\
& 0 \le \alpha_k \le C_k \tau, \quad \forall k \\
& 0 \le \alpha_k^* \le C_k (1-\tau), \quad \forall k
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the dual matrix $\mathbf{Q}_{dual}$ and vector $\mathbf{p}_{dual}$ are defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dual_matrices}
\mathbf{Q}_{dual} = \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1} \boldsymbol{K}, \quad \mathbf{p}_{dual} = \lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1} \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{Y} - \boldsymbol{Y}
\end{equation}
Once the optimal $\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*$ are found using a QP solver for Eq. (\ref{eq:dual_problem}), we can recover $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ using Eq. (\ref{eq:beta_optimal}) and construct the final decision function $f(\boldsymbol{x})$.


\subsection{The Algorithm and Complexity Analysis}\label{alg_section}
The complete training process of the proposed TLQLDMR model is summarized in Algorithm \ref{alg:TLQLDMR}. The algorithm mainly consists of matrix construction and solving a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem.

\begin{algorithm}
\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}
\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}
\caption{TLQLDMR Training Algorithm}
\label{alg:TLQLDMR}
\Input{Source Data $D_S = \{(\boldsymbol{x}_{S,i}, y_{S,i})\}_{i=1}^{n_S}$, Target Data $D_T = \{(\boldsymbol{x}_{T,j}, y_{T,j})\}_{j=1}^{n_T}$, Quantile $\tau$, Kernel parameters, Regularization parameters $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, Pinball Loss weights $C_S, C_T$.}
\Output{Optimal dual variables $\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*$ and bias $b$.}

\tcp{Step 1: Data Preparation}
Combine Source and Target data: $\boldsymbol{X} = [\boldsymbol{X}_S, \boldsymbol{X}_T]$, $\boldsymbol{Y} = [\boldsymbol{Y}_S; \boldsymbol{Y}_T]$, $N = n_S + n_T$\;
Set weights vector $\boldsymbol{C} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ where $C_k = C_S$ for $k \le n_S$ and $C_T$ otherwise\;

\tcp{Step 2: Matrix Construction}
Compute Kernel Matrix $\boldsymbol{K} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ using the specified kernel function\;
Construct Variance Matrix $\mathbf{S}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:matrix_S}\;
Construct MMD Matrix $\mathbf{M}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:matrix_M}\;

\tcp{Step 3: Dual Problem Formulation}
Compute coefficient matrix $\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \boldsymbol{K} + \lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{K} + \lambda_2 \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{M} \boldsymbol{K}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:Omega}\;
Invert $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ to fit the dual form\;
Compute Dual Matrix $\mathbf{Q}_{dual} = \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1} \boldsymbol{K}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:dual_matrices}\;
Compute Dual Vector $\mathbf{p}_{dual} = \lambda_1 \boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1} \boldsymbol{K} \mathbf{S} \boldsymbol{Y} - \boldsymbol{Y}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:dual_matrices}\;

\tcp{Step 4: Solve QP}
Solve the QP problem in Eq. \eqref{eq:dual_problem} providing $\mathbf{Q}_{dual}$, $\mathbf{p}_{dual}$, and constraints $0 \le \alpha_k \le C_k\tau$, $0 \le \alpha_k^* \le C_k(1-\tau)$\;
\textbf{Return} Optimal $\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*$\;

\tcp{Step 5: Recover Primal Variables}
Compute $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \boldsymbol{\alpha} - \boldsymbol{\alpha}^*$\;
Recover $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:beta_optimal}\;
Compute bias $b$ using complementary slackness conditions (averaged over Support Vectors)\;
\end{algorithm}

The computational complexity of TLQLDMR primarily stems from three parts: matrix multiplication, matrix inversion, and the QP solver.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Matrix Operations}: The construction of $\boldsymbol{K}$, $\mathbf{S}$, and $\mathbf{M}$ involves $O(N^2 d)$ or $O(N^2)$ operations. The calculation of $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ involves matrix multiplications of size $N \times N$, leading to a complexity of $O(N^3)$.
    \item \textbf{Matrix Inversion}: Inverting the $N \times N$ matrix $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ requires $O(N^3)$ complexity.
    \item \textbf{QP Solver}: Solving the standard Quadratic Programming problem for the dual variables typically has a complexity ranging from $O(N^{2.3})$ to $O(N^3)$ depending on the solver implementation (e.g., Interior Point Method).
\end{enumerate}
Therefore, the overall time complexity of the proposed algorithm is approximately $O(N^3)$. Given that $N = n_S + n_T$ and $n_T \ll n_S$, the scale is dominated by the source domain sample size. While this complexity is acceptable for moderate-sized datasets, it becomes prohibitive for large-scale wind power forecasting scenarios involving tens of thousands of samples. To overcome this computational bottleneck and enable practical deployment in real-world wind farm applications, we develop a scalable optimization algorithm in the following subsection.

\subsection{Scalable Optimization via Nystr\"{o}m Approximation}\label{fast_alg}
To address the computational bottleneck of the standard QP-based solver, we propose a scalable optimization algorithm that combines Nystr\"{o}m approximation with stochastic gradient descent. This approach reduces the time complexity from $O(N^3)$ to $O(T \cdot N \cdot m)$, where $T$ denotes the number of iterations and $m \ll N$ is the number of landmark points.

\subsubsection{Nystr\"{o}m Feature Mapping}
The core idea is to approximate the implicit infinite-dimensional feature mapping $\phi(\boldsymbol{x})$ with an explicit finite-dimensional representation. Given the training data, we first select $m$ landmark points $Z = \{z_1, \ldots, z_m\}$ from the source domain $D_S$ using K-Means clustering to capture the underlying data manifold.

Let $\boldsymbol{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ denote the kernel matrix of landmarks, where $W_{ij} = k(z_i, z_j)$. The explicit feature mapping $\psi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is then constructed as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nystrom_feature}
\psi(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{W}^{-1/2} [k(\boldsymbol{x}, z_1), \ldots, k(\boldsymbol{x}, z_m)]^T
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{W}^{-1/2}$ is computed via eigendecomposition. This mapping satisfies the approximation property $k(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j) \approx \psi(\boldsymbol{x}_i)^T \psi(\boldsymbol{x}_j)$, enabling us to work directly in the primal space.

\subsubsection{Primal Formulation with Explicit Features}
With the explicit feature mapping, the prediction function becomes $f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \boldsymbol{w}^T \psi(\boldsymbol{x}) + b$, where $\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Let $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_S \in \mathbb{R}^{n_S \times m}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_T \in \mathbb{R}^{n_T \times m}$ denote the mapped feature matrices for source and target domains, respectively. The regularization matrix $\boldsymbol{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ incorporating structural risk, variance, and MMD terms is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reg_matrix}
\boldsymbol{R} = \boldsymbol{I} + \lambda_1 \underbrace{\boldsymbol{\Psi}_S^T \boldsymbol{H}_S \boldsymbol{\Psi}_S}_{\text{Variance}} + \lambda_2 \underbrace{(\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_S - \bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_T)(\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_S - \bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_T)^T}_{\text{MMD}}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{H}_S = \boldsymbol{I}_{n_S} - \frac{1}{n_S} \boldsymbol{1}_{n_S} \boldsymbol{1}_{n_S}^T$ is the centering matrix, and $\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_S$, $\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_T$ are the mean feature vectors of source and target domains.

\subsubsection{Stochastic Gradient Descent Optimization}
Instead of solving the dual QP problem, we directly optimize the primal objective using mini-batch stochastic gradient descent with the Adam optimizer. For each sample $k$ in a mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$, the sub-gradient of the Pinball Loss with respect to the prediction is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:subgradient}
g_k = \frac{\partial L_{\tau}}{\partial \hat{y}_k} = \begin{cases} 
\tau - 1, & \text{if } y_k < \hat{y}_k \\ 
\tau, & \text{if } y_k \ge \hat{y}_k
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The weighted sub-gradient is computed as $\tilde{g}_k = C_k \cdot g_k$, where $C_k \in \{C_S, C_T\}$ depends on the domain membership. The parameter gradients are then:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:param_gradients}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}} J = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{B}} \tilde{g}_k \psi(\boldsymbol{x}_k) + \boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{w}, \quad \nabla_b J = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{B}} \tilde{g}_k
\end{equation}

The complete training procedure is summarized in Algorithm \ref{alg:fast_TLQLDMR}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}
\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}
\caption{Fast TLQLDMR via Nystr\"{o}m Approximation}
\label{alg:fast_TLQLDMR}
\Input{Source data $D_S$, Target data $D_T$, Number of landmarks $m$, Quantile $\tau$, Hyperparameters $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, C_S, C_T$, Learning rate $\eta$, Batch size $B$, Max iterations $T$.}
\Output{Optimal weights $\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ and bias $b \in \mathbb{R}$.}

\tcp{Step 1: Nystr\"{o}m Feature Mapping}
Select $m$ landmarks $Z = \{z_1, \ldots, z_m\}$ from $D_S$ via K-Means\;
Compute landmark kernel matrix $\boldsymbol{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$\;
Compute $\boldsymbol{W}^{-1/2}$ via eigendecomposition\;
Construct explicit features $\psi(\boldsymbol{x})$ for all samples using Eq. \eqref{eq:nystrom_feature}\;

\tcp{Step 2: Pre-compute Regularization Matrix}
Compute feature matrices $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_S$, $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_T$ and mean vectors $\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_S$, $\bar{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_T$\;
Construct regularization matrix $\boldsymbol{R}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:reg_matrix}\;

\tcp{Step 3: Stochastic Optimization}
Initialize $\boldsymbol{w} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{0}$, $b \leftarrow 0$\;
\For{$t = 1$ \KwTo $T$}{
    Shuffle combined dataset $D = D_S \cup D_T$\;
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B} \subset D$}{
        Compute predictions: $\hat{y}_k = \boldsymbol{w}^T \psi(\boldsymbol{x}_k) + b$, $\forall k \in \mathcal{B}$\;
        Compute sub-gradients $g_k$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:subgradient}\;
        Apply sample weights: $\tilde{g}_k \leftarrow C_k \cdot g_k$\;
        Compute gradients $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}} J$, $\nabla_b J$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:param_gradients}\;
        Update $\boldsymbol{w}$, $b$ using Adam optimizer with learning rate $\eta$\;
    }
}
\Return $\boldsymbol{w}$, $b$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Complexity Analysis of the Scalable Algorithm}
The proposed scalable algorithm achieves significant computational savings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Landmark Selection}: K-Means clustering requires $O(N \cdot m \cdot I_{km})$ operations, where $I_{km}$ is the number of K-Means iterations.
    \item \textbf{Feature Mapping}: Computing $\boldsymbol{W}^{-1/2}$ costs $O(m^3)$, and mapping all $N$ samples requires $O(N \cdot m \cdot d)$.
    \item \textbf{Regularization Matrix}: Constructing $\boldsymbol{R}$ involves $O(n_S \cdot m^2)$ for the variance term and $O(m^2)$ for the MMD term.
    \item \textbf{SGD Iterations}: Each iteration processes $O(N)$ samples with $O(m)$ operations per sample, yielding $O(T \cdot N \cdot m)$ total complexity.
\end{enumerate}

The overall time complexity is $O(T \cdot N \cdot m + m^3)$. Since $m \ll N$ (typically $m = O(\sqrt{N})$), this represents a substantial reduction from the $O(N^3)$ complexity of the standard QP solver. Table \ref{tab:complexity} summarizes the comparison between the two algorithms.

\subsubsection{Theoretical Justification}
The convergence of the proposed stochastic optimization algorithm is guaranteed under standard assumptions. Since the Pinball Loss is convex and Lipschitz continuous, and the regularization term $\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{w}^T \boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{w}$ is strongly convex when $\boldsymbol{R}$ is positive definite, the overall objective function is strongly convex. According to the convergence theory of SGD for strongly convex functions \cite{bottou2018optimization}, the algorithm converges to the global optimum at a rate of $O(1/T)$.

Furthermore, the Nystr\"{o}m approximation error is bounded. Let $\tilde{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j) = \psi(\boldsymbol{x}_i)^T \psi(\boldsymbol{x}_j)$ denote the approximated kernel. Under mild conditions on the kernel eigenvalue decay, the approximation error satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nystrom_error}
\mathbb{E}\left[ \| \boldsymbol{K} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{K}} \|_F \right] \le O\left( \frac{N}{\sqrt{m}} \right)
\end{equation}
This bound ensures that with a sufficient number of landmarks, the approximation quality is well-controlled, and the solution obtained by the scalable algorithm closely approximates that of the exact QP solver.

\section{Conclusion}\label{Conclu}
Accurate wind power prediction under extreme meteorological conditions is crucial yet methodologically arduous due to extreme sample imbalance, drastic distribution shifts, and the probabilistic necessity of capturing highly asymmetric tail volatility. To overcome the inherent deficiencies of symmetric large margin algorithms and conventional unsupervised feature alignment techniques, this paper proposed the Transfer Learning Quantile Large Margin Distribution Machine for Regression (TLQLDMR).

Extensive evaluations on public operational wind farm SCADA datasets rigorously validated the TLQLDMR framework's deterministic accuracy and probabilistic interval forecasting competence. First, its dual-space domain adaptation framework---unifying MMD-based feature alignment with an asymmetric weighted Pinball Objective---demonstrated unambiguous superiority over contemporaneous baseline algorithms, yielding definitive point prediction dominance under volatile measurement noise settings. Second, through carefully tuned interval scaling validation, TLQLDMR systematically achieved nominal 95\% prediction interval coverage constraints while preserving the sharpest aggregate calibration boundaries (PINAW = 0.268, CWC = 0.268) amidst severe meteorological conditions. Third, systematic ablation analyses isolated and confirmed that the holistic fusion of cross-domain knowledge transposition, distributional variance compacting, and feature proximity alignment is strictly indispensable for managing heavy-tailed prediction fluctuations. Lastly, implementing a Nystr\"{o}m-approximated Stochastic Gradient Descent optimization routine conclusively dissolved typical kernel complexity limitations, fostering manageable scalability on vast sensor data histories previously intractable for rigorous analytical quadratic programming.

Looking forward, the extension of the proposed asymmetric variance constraints to broader spatiotemporal correlation tasks and its integration with sophisticated deep self-attention encoders present promising avenues toward fully adaptive sustainable energy intelligence.
