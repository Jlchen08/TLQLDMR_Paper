\section{Experiments}\label{Experiments}

In this section, we conduct a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the proposed TL-QLDMR model for wind power forecasting under extreme weather conditions. The experiments are organized into five progressive stages: (1) extreme weather sample screening and domain shift quantification across multiple wind farms (Experiment~1); (2) deterministic point prediction comparison on the wind farm exhibiting the most severe distribution shift (Experiment~2); (3) probabilistic interval prediction evaluation under a 95\% confidence level (Experiment~3); (4) ablation study to validate the contribution of each core component (Experiment~4); and (5) scalable optimization validation to assess the computational efficiency of the proposed Nystr\"{o}m + SGD solver (Experiment~5). We first describe the dataset and experimental setup in Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:setup}, then present the results and analysis in Sections~\ref{sec:exp1}--\ref{sec:exp5}.

%---------------- Dataset Description --------------------%
\subsection{Dataset Description}\label{sec:data}

The experiments are conducted on a publicly available wind farm SCADA dataset published by the State Grid Renewable Energy Generation Dataset \citep{s41597-022-01696-6}. The dataset contains supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) records from six onshore wind farms with nominal capacities ranging from 36\,MW to 200\,MW. Each record is sampled at 15-minute intervals and includes the following meteorological and operational variables:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Wind speed at multiple heights: hub height, 50\,m, 30\,m, and 10\,m (m/s);
    \item Wind direction at hub height (degrees);
    \item Air temperature ($^\circ$C);
    \item Atmospheric pressure (hPa);
    \item Relative humidity (\%);
    \item Active power output (MW).
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:farm_summary} summarizes the basic characteristics of the six wind farms. Each farm contains approximately 70{,}000 time-stamped records spanning multiple years of continuous operation.

%---------------- Feature Engineering --------------------%
\subsection{Feature Engineering and Data Preprocessing}\label{sec:features}

The feature set comprises multi-height wind speeds, air temperature, atmospheric pressure, relative humidity, and cyclically encoded wind direction. Specifically, the raw wind direction angle $\theta$ is transformed into two orthogonal components $\sin(\theta)$ and $\cos(\theta)$ to eliminate the discontinuity at the $0^\circ/360^\circ$ boundary, which is a well-established practice in meteorological feature engineering \citep{wang2019review}.

A sliding window of length $W = 24$ (corresponding to a 6-hour lookback horizon at 15-minute resolution) is applied to construct supervised learning samples. For each window, the feature matrix $\boldsymbol{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{W \times d}$ is flattened into a vector $\boldsymbol{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{Wd}$, and the target variable $y_t$ is the power output at the next time step. All features and the target variable are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using statistics computed from the training set. Missing values are imputed with the per-feature mean to ensure numerical stability for kernel-based models.

%---------------- Extreme Weather Definition --------------------%
\subsection{Extreme Weather Event Definition}\label{sec:extreme_def}

A critical prerequisite for evaluating transfer learning under extreme conditions is the rigorous definition of what constitutes an ``extreme weather event.'' We adopt a rule-based identification scheme that combines statistical thresholds with physical domain knowledge. A sample is classified as belonging to the \textit{target domain} (extreme weather) if it satisfies any of the following criteria:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Statistical wind speed extreme}: Hub-height wind speed exceeds the 98th percentile of the farm's historical distribution;
    \item \textbf{Temperature extreme}: Air temperature falls below the 2nd percentile or exceeds the 98th percentile;
    \item \textbf{Cut-out wind speed}: Hub-height wind speed exceeds 25\,m/s, the typical cut-out threshold for modern wind turbines.
\end{enumerate}

Notably, the power ramp criterion (rapid power fluctuations exceeding 5\% of nominal capacity per 15-minute interval) is deliberately excluded from the extreme definition. This design choice avoids conflating control-induced power curtailment or dispatch adjustments with genuinely weather-driven anomalies. The union of the above three rules yields a target domain that is both physically interpretable and statistically sufficient for model training. All remaining samples constitute the \textit{source domain} (normal weather conditions).

%---------------- Experimental Setup --------------------%
\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

For deterministic point prediction, we employ Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) as evaluation metrics. RMSE and MAE quantify the magnitude of prediction errors, while $R^2$ measures the proportion of variance explained by the model. For probabilistic interval prediction, we adopt Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP), Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW), Prediction Interval Normalized Average Width (PINAW), Coverage Width-based Criterion (CWC), and Winkler Score. PICP measures reliability (whether the interval achieves the target coverage), while MPIW and PINAW measure sharpness (how tight the interval is). CWC is a composite metric that penalizes PINAW when PICP falls below the nominal coverage $1 - \alpha$, and the Winkler Score combines interval width with a penalty proportional to the magnitude of coverage violations. An ideal model achieves PICP $\ge 1 - \alpha$ with the smallest possible PINAW.

To quantify the distribution discrepancy between normal and extreme domains, we employ two complementary metrics computed on both wind speed and power dimensions: the Wasserstein-1 distance ($W_1$), which measures the minimum transportation cost to transform one distribution into the other, and the Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic (KS), which captures the supremum of the absolute difference between the empirical cumulative distribution functions of the two domains.

All experiments are implemented in Python using NumPy and scikit-learn. For SVM-based models, the training sample size is capped at 4{,}000 source domain samples and 1{,}200 target domain samples to maintain tractable computation times for kernel methods. Tree-based and ensemble models use the full training set without subsampling. The TL-QLDMR model employs a Nystr\"{o}m approximation with $m = 1{,}200$ landmark points and is optimized via stochastic gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.01 for 64 epochs and a mini-batch size of 256. All random splits use a fixed seed for reproducibility.

%---------------- Experiment 2 --------------------%
\subsection{Experiment 2: Deterministic Point Prediction under Extreme Weather}\label{sec:exp2}

The second experiment evaluates deterministic point prediction on Farm~6 and compares TL-QLDMR with nine baselines under the same data split and preprocessing protocol.

The data partition for Farm~6 is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Source domain} (normal weather): 66{,}009 samples;
    \item \textbf{Target domain} (extreme weather): 4{,}143 samples.
\end{itemize}

We compare TL-QLDMR against classical kernel baselines, robust kernel variants, and representative ensemble methods for wind power forecasting:

\textbf{Classical and robust kernel baselines:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standard SVR}: Classical Support Vector Regression with RBF kernel and $\epsilon$-insensitive loss, serving as the foundational kernel-based regression baseline;
    \item \textbf{Standard LDMR}: Large-margin Distribution Machine-based Regression with symmetric variance regularization, representing the direct predecessor of TL-QLDMR without transfer learning or asymmetric modifications;
    \item \textbf{BLSSVR} \citep{fu2023bounded}: A bounded-loss SVR-type regression model designed to improve robustness against outliers and heavy-tailed disturbances.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Additional SVM variants:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{3}
    \item \textbf{HHO-SVR} \citep{houssein2025hho}: Support Vector Regression with hyperparameters optimized by the Harris Hawks Optimization metaheuristic;
    \item \textbf{FLSVR} \citep{meena2025flsvr}: A functional iterative method for solving the Lagrangian dual of SVR, offering improved convergence properties;
    \item \textbf{ARA-SVR} \citep{duan2025ara}: SVR with Associated Road Analysis for feature selection, originally proposed for short-term traffic flow prediction.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Ensemble methods:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{6}
    \item \textbf{KMeans-GBT} \citep{rizkallah2025kmeans}: A two-stage model combining KMeans clustering with Gradient Boosted Trees to capture heterogeneous input--output relationships;
    \item \textbf{RF-WPF} \citep{mustaffa2025rf}: A Random Forest model tailored for wind power forecasting with optimized tree depth and feature subsampling;
    \item \textbf{BRF-WPF} \citep{chen2025brf}: Broad Random Forest integrating Broad Learning System feature expansion with Random Forest regression for short-term wind power prediction.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:exp2_results} and Figs.~\ref{fig:exp2_curves_a}--\ref{fig:exp2_curves_e} jointly demonstrate that TL-QLDMR provides the most accurate and stable point forecasts on the Farm~6 extreme-weather test set. Several observations are noteworthy:

First, comparing TL-QLDMR against classical and robust kernel baselines reveals the value of the proposed extensions. Standard SVR achieves moderate performance (RMSE = 8.564, $R^2$ = 0.9413), establishing a solid baseline for kernel-based regression. However, Standard LDMR, despite incorporating variance regularization for distributional robustness, exhibits the weakest performance among kernel methods (RMSE = 10.692, $R^2$ = 0.9085). BLSSVR, although designed to improve robustness via bounded loss, improves only marginally over Standard LDMR (RMSE = 10.273, $R^2$ = 0.9155) and remains clearly inferior to Standard SVR and HHO-SVR. This pattern confirms our theoretical analysis in Section~\ref{novel}: sample-wise robustness alone is insufficient in the cross-domain extreme weather scenario where (i) training and testing distributions differ substantially, and (ii) the model must adapt to target-domain dynamics. The large performance gaps from Standard LDMR and BLSSVR to TL-QLDMR quantitatively validate the necessity of jointly incorporating transfer learning and asymmetric variance regularization.

Second, TL-QLDMR improves on the strongest non-transfer baselines, including HHO-SVR and RF-WPF. Relative to HHO-SVR, TL-QLDMR raises $R^2$ from 0.9537 to 0.9557 and reduces RMSE from 7.603 to 7.478. It also improves on RF-WPF, the strongest ensemble comparator, reducing RMSE from 7.967 to 7.478. Although the numerical margin over HHO-SVR is moderate, it is obtained under the most severe domain-shift setting with only 4{,}143 extreme-weather samples. The segment-level evidence in Figs.~\ref{fig:exp2_curves_a}--\ref{fig:exp2_curves_e} is consistent with the aggregate metrics: TL-QLDMR tracks abrupt ramps and low-power transitions more closely, while weaker kernel baselines such as Standard LDMR and BLSSVR show larger local deviations around rapid fluctuations.

Third, among baselines, HHO-SVR remains the most competitive kernel method, RF-WPF remains the strongest non-kernel alternative, and BLSSVR does not translate its bounded-loss robustness into competitive cross-domain accuracy under the present small-sample extreme-weather regime. The observed gain of TL-QLDMR is consistent with the combined effect of source-domain transfer, asymmetric target weighting, and cyclic wind-direction encoding. Because many extreme-weather periods contain near-zero power outputs, MAPE is numerically unstable and therefore RMSE and $R^2$ are treated as the principal deterministic indicators.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Probabilistic Interval Prediction under Extreme Weather}\label{sec:exp3}

The third experiment extends evaluation from point prediction to probabilistic interval forecasting. The objective is to verify whether TL-QLDMR can achieve reliable 95\% prediction intervals while keeping interval width sufficiently sharp under extreme-weather volatility.

The interval prediction baselines are organized into two categories:

\textbf{Kernel-based quantile and robust learning variants:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standard SVQR}: Classical Support Vector Quantile Regression with pinball loss, serving as the foundational kernel-based interval prediction baseline;
    \item \textbf{TSVQR} \citep{tsvqr2023}: Twin Support Vector Quantile Regression, which constructs separate hyperplanes for upper and lower quantiles;
    \item \textbf{UQSVM-SSVQR} \citep{wang2024ssvqr}: A robust SVQR variant with truncated pinball loss, used as a strong SVM-based interval baseline;
    \item \textbf{NFS-SVQR} \citep{ye2025nfs}: Nonlinear Feature Selection combined with SVQR, published in \textit{Neural Networks} (2025);
    \item \textbf{NuSVR-CI} \citep{scholkopf2000new,romano2019cqr}: $\nu$-Support Vector Regression with conformalized quantile regression (CQR)-calibrated prediction intervals, included as a classical SVM baseline;
    \item \textbf{RLMKL} \citep{jiang2021rlmkl}: Robust low-rank multiple kernel learning with compound regularization; its quantile-loss instantiation is used here as a strong robust kernel interval baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Recent wind power interval prediction models:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{6}
    \item \textbf{HybridDL-Interval} \citep{hybriddl2025}: A hybrid deep learning framework for day-ahead wind power interval forecasting, combining temporal attention and quantile loss (approximate implementation);
    \item \textbf{AMQRNN} \citep{amqrnn2025}: A recurrent attention encoder-decoder network for multi-step interval wind power prediction, implemented using an attention-GRU network;
    \item \textbf{QGBR} \citep{hu2025ncqgbr}: Quantile Gradient Boosted Regression, representing the improved quantile ensemble learning framework for wind power prediction;
    \item \textbf{CQR-MLP} \citep{hu2025nescqr}: MLP quantile ensemble with dynamic conformalized quantile regression, acting as a simplified baseline for neural ensemble search methods.
\end{enumerate}

% For interval prediction, TL-QLDMR trains two separate quantile models at $\tau_{\text{low}}$ and $\tau_{\text{high}}$ to construct the lower and upper bounds of the prediction interval. The quantile pair $(\tau_{\text{low}}=0.2, \tau_{\text{high}}=0.8)$ is jointly selected with the hyperparameters on the validation set, using a coverage-priority criterion that first ensures PICP approaches the target coverage $0.95 + \delta$ and then minimizes PINAW via a learned interval scaling factor (width\_scale). The resulting width scaling for TL-QLDMR is 0.88, which yields reliable coverage while avoiding unnecessary interval inflation.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Ablation Study}\label{sec:exp4}

To validate the contribution of the three core components (transfer learning, LDMR variance regularization, and MMD feature alignment), this ablation experiment evaluates structurally reduced variants on both point and interval tasks. Additionally, to verify the necessity of asymmetric variance regularization for quantile regression, we include LDMR-Quantile (SymVar), which replaces the asymmetric variance term with standard symmetric variance minimization while retaining the transfer learning framework.

The full TL-QLDMR framework is compared against four architectural reductions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{LDMR-Quantile (SymVar)}: Replaces asymmetric variance regularization with symmetric variance minimization, testing whether the asymmetry is necessary for quantile-specific calibration.
    \item \textbf{w/o Transfer (Target-only QLDMR)}: Trained exclusively on extreme weather target samples without source domain integration or MMD alignment.
    \item \textbf{w/o LDMR (Variance Penalty)}: The asymmetric variance regularizer is removed, deteriorating the framework into a generalized Transfer SVR/SVQR variant that lacks distributional robustness.
    \item \textbf{w/o MMD (Feature Alignment)}: The algorithm employs cross-domain training data but disables Maximum Mean Discrepancy alignment, relying purely on weighted objective loss.
\end{enumerate}

%---------------- Discussion --------------------%
\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The extended experimental evaluations yield the following conclusive insights:

\textbf{Classical and robust kernel baselines establish the necessity of proposed extensions.} Experiment~2 demonstrates that Standard SVR achieves moderate performance (RMSE = 8.564), while Standard LDMR and BLSSVR remain substantially weaker on the cross-domain extreme-weather task. This degradation confirms that symmetric variance minimization or bounded-loss robustness alone is insufficient when target-domain adaptation is absent. In Experiment~3, RLMKL provides a stronger robust-kernel interval baseline than conventional SVQR variants, but TL-QLDMR still achieves markedly better joint reliability-sharpness quality, showing that asymmetric variance regularization and supervised transfer adaptation remain necessary.

\textbf{Transfer learning improves deterministic forecasting under severe shift.} Experiment~2 shows consistent gains in RMSE and $R^2$ over both classical baselines and recent variants, and curve-level evidence confirms better tracking during abrupt peak-valley transitions.

\textbf{Interval forecasting achieves a better reliability-sharpness balance.} In Experiment~3, TL-QLDMR achieves the highest coverage reliability among the compared methods while also obtaining the best composite penalty (CWC), and this advantage persists under injected measurement noise.

\textbf{Ablation confirms the necessity of each component.} Removing transfer learning reduces both point and interval performance under severe domain shift; removing asymmetric LDMR variance regularization degrades distributional robustness and interval calibration; removing MMD alignment weakens feature-space adaptation. Critically, the LDMR-Quantile (SymVar) variant confirms that symmetric variance minimization conflicts with quantile-specific asymmetry requirements, validating our theoretical analysis. Residual-density plots provide complementary evidence for this degradation.

\textbf{The scalable optimizer materially reduces computational burden.} Experiment~5 verifies that the Nystr\"{o}m + SGD formulation remains practical in regimes where full QP optimization fails due to computational constraints.

\textbf{Limitations and future work.} The present evaluation is based on onshore wind farms and fixed feature engineering pipelines; extension to offshore assets, dynamic re-calibration strategies, and hybrid deep-kernel transfer formulations remains a relevant direction.
